CHAPTER 01

Start here: you are buying a business

An apartment building is both a physical asset and a business that sells a place to live.

Residents pay rent. The owner pays to keep the property safe, livable, staffed, insured and legally compliant. A lender may supply part of the purchase money. Investors supply the rest. Whatever remains after bills, loan payments and necessary cash savings may be available to investors.

Buildings with five or more residential units are generally financed as commercial multifamily. Two-to-four-unit properties generally follow residential financing rules. The distinction affects how lenders assess the property and how the loan works.

The central question: Can this property reliably collect enough money to cover its real costs, survive problems, repay its debt and compensate investors for the risk?

Three things have to work together: the property must serve real demand; the financing must fit the cash flow; and the people and ownership terms must protect and fairly compensate your investment. A good building can still be a bad investment at the wrong price or with the wrong loan.

Facts, estimates and promises

A fact has evidence: a signed lease, paid invoice or bank deposit. An estimate is a reasonable guess about an uncertain outcome. A promise is a contractual obligation, whose value still depends on the ability to perform.

Why it matters: Keep these separate. A spreadsheet number does not become a fact because it has two decimal places.

Ask: What document supports this number, and what happens if it is wrong?

Actual, current and stabilized

Actual describes a past period. Current describes today, sometimes annualized. Stabilized describes an expected steady operating condition after changes.

Why it matters: A stabilized return may take months or years to achieve. You pay the bills during the transition.

Ask: When does stabilization occur, what does it cost, and how is the early cash shortage funded?

Underwriting

Underwriting means building your own reasoned estimate of income, costs, debt and returns using evidence.

Why it matters: It turns a sales story into an investment decision. You need a base case, a downside case and enough cash to get through the downside.

Ask: Which assumptions drive the result, and how much room is there for error?

A sensible first reading order

  1. Identify what is being sold, where it is, its unit count and asking price.
  2. Read the rent roll for today’s leasing situation.
  3. Read the operating statement for money actually earned and spent.
  4. Trace unusual income and expenses to detailed evidence.
  5. Compare the forecast with history and ask what must change.
  6. Add financing, repair costs, reserves, fees and ownership terms.
  7. Test a disappointing outcome before deciding to proceed.

Dates matter: a rent roll from July, a T12 ending June and a September OM describe different moments. Create a document-date list before comparing them. Request updated records through closing.

CHAPTER 03

Who does what—and who gets paid

Know whose interests you are relying on.

Seller and broker

The seller owns the property. The broker markets it and helps negotiate the sale; representation depends on the engagement.

Why it matters: A selling broker has a reason to emphasize upside. Helpful information still needs verification.

Ask: Who does the broker represent, and who pays the commission?

Sponsor / general partner (GP)

The sponsor organizes the investment, finds the property, arranges debt and oversees the business plan. GP is commonly used for the managing party even when the entity is an LLC.

Why it matters: Execution depends on their judgment, resources and incentives.

Ask: What happened on similar completed deals, including deals that missed projections? How much of their own cash is invested?

Limited partner (LP)

An LP is generally a passive investor who contributes money and has limited control. Exact rights depend on the entity documents.

Why it matters: Your ability to fix a problem or retrieve your money may be limited.

Ask: What can investors vote on? Can the manager be removed? Can more money be required?

Property manager and asset manager

The property manager handles residents, leasing, rent collection and daily repairs. The asset manager supervises performance, budget, financing and strategy.

Why it matters: These are different jobs and may create separate fees. Staffing quality affects both income and expenses.

Ask: What is each fee based on? Who handles after-hours issues, bookkeeping, leasing and construction?

Lender and guarantor

The lender supplies debt. A loan guarantor accepts specified obligations if things go wrong. This is different from a tenant-rent guarantee provider.

Why it matters: The lender is paid ahead of equity. A guaranty can create exposure beyond your original cash investment.

Ask: Who signs which guaranties, and what obligations survive a sale or default?

Inspection and professional team

Inspectors, engineers, environmental consultants, attorneys, accountants and insurance advisors help verify specialized issues.

Why it matters: Each report has a scope and exclusions. A general inspection does not establish every legal or engineering fact.

Ask: Which issue does each person own, what is excluded and what remains unresolved?

CHAPTER 04

Read a rent roll without getting fooled

A rented unit, a signed lease and a paying resident are three different facts.

Unit mix and weighted average rent

Unit mix is the count of studios, one-bedrooms, two-bedrooms and other layouts. A weighted average accounts for how many units exist at each rent. Example: 10 units at $800 and 30 at $1,200 average $1,100, not $1,000.

Why it matters: Different sizes attract different residents and achievable rents. A portfolio average can hide a struggling building.

Ask: Are rents averaged over occupied units only or all units? Are utilities or other charges included?

Lease dates and status

Read the start date, end date, current rent, concessions and renewal terms. An expired lease may be month-to-month; a future start date may be pre-leasing. Verify both.

Why it matters: Lease expiration clusters can cause simultaneous vacancy and repair costs. A future lease is not current occupancy.

Ask: Which leases are actually in effect, who has given notice, and which leases expire each month?

Vacant versus down units

Vacant means no resident occupies the unit. Down means the unit is not currently rentable, often because of damage, repair or legal issues.

Why it matters: A down unit cannot produce rent simply by advertising it. Count its repair cost and downtime.

Ask: How many units are ready now, and what work and money are needed for each remaining unit?

Physical versus economic occupancy

Physical occupancy measures units occupied divided by total units. Economic occupancy measures realized rental revenue relative to a defined potential-rent amount. Definitions vary; confirm the denominator.

Why it matters: A full building with unpaid rent can produce weak cash flow. Unit occupancy also differs from rent-weighted vacancy when rents vary.

Ask: Show occupied units, paying units, actual collections, unpaid balances and concessions separately.

Delinquency and bad debt

Delinquency is rent owed but unpaid. An aging report sorts it by how long it has been overdue. Bad debt is an amount judged uncollectible or written off.

Why it matters: Old unpaid balances should not be treated like cash in the bank. Collections from former residents are not current occupancy.

Ask: How much is 30, 60 or 90-plus days overdue? What was actually collected afterward?

Security deposits

A refundable deposit is money held against resident obligations. It is generally a liability until properly applied, not ordinary rental income. Handling and transfer depend on applicable rules.

Why it matters: If you take over the obligation but do not receive the funds or credit, you may fund future refunds yourself.

Ask: Do lease deposits, tenant ledgers, bank balances and closing credits reconcile?

Example: 40 apartments, 36 occupied = 90% physical occupancy. If full potential rent is $48,000 monthly and collected rent is $38,400, cash-based economic occupancy on that definition is 80%. The missing money needs an explanation: vacant units, free rent, lower contractual rents or unpaid charges. Avoid deducting the same loss twice.

NOI is not your take-home money. It helps compare properties before financing. Cash available reflects financing and additional cash needs. Taxable income is a different number again.

Conventions vary. Some statements begin with actual rental revenue and never display GPR. Some deduct replacement reserves in lender net cash flow below NOI. Read definitions before comparing ratios. Never subtract vacancy again from rent that already reflects vacancy.

Utility reimbursement: gross versus net

If the owner pays $30,000 in utilities and collects $18,000 from residents, the net cost is $12,000. You can show both gross amounts or the net cost, but do not show $18,000 income and only $12,000 expense: that double-counts the benefit. RUBS means ratio utility billing system, which allocates a shared utility bill using a formula. Verify lease authority, local rules, recoverability, billing cost and actual collections.

Cash versus accrual accounting

Cash accounting generally records revenue when money arrives and expenses when paid. Accrual generally records revenue when earned and expenses when incurred. A late payment or unpaid repair bill can make the same business look different under the two methods. Ask which method produced the P&L and reconcile receivables, payables and bank activity.

CHAPTER 06

The T12: turn history into a realistic budget

A historical statement is a starting point. Your ownership costs may be different.

Monthly trends and seasonality

A true monthly T12 shows the last twelve individual months. Annual totals alone cannot reveal whether collections recently weakened or repairs occurred in one month. T3 means the last three months.

Why it matters: Multiplying a strong recent month by twelve can exaggerate income or miss winter costs.

Ask: Provide monthly revenue, occupancy, collections and expenses for at least the T12, with more history if available.

Normalization

Normalization means adjusting history into a supportable ongoing budget: remove a genuinely nonrecurring event, add an omitted recurring expense or replace a seller-specific cost with your quoted cost.

Why it matters: Every adjustment changes NOI and often the implied price. It should have evidence and a matching cash plan.

Ask: Show historical amount, adjustment, reason, evidence and the resulting budget for each line.

Property taxes and insurance

These are operating expenses. A sale, assessment change, policy change, deductible or lender requirement may change the amount.

Why it matters: Last year’s bill may understate what you will pay. Ordinary inflation assumptions do not explain a known reset.

Ask: Get a local tax estimate and a bindable insurance quote, including deductibles and exclusions.

Management, payroll and administration

Include leasing, management, payroll and benefits, software, office, accounting, licensing and legal costs as appropriate. Fees may be based on billed revenue, collected revenue or another base.

Why it matters: Owner self-management does not mean these jobs are free for the buyer.

Ask: What services are included, what is extra and do the fee percentages reproduce the dollar amounts?

Repairs and recurring services

Repairs, cleaning, turns, security, pest control, landscaping, trash and routine system maintenance support continued rent collection.

Why it matters: A forecast that cuts these costs may assume problems disappear without paying to solve them.

Ask: Were costs buried in another category? Which reductions have bids, contracts or operating proof?

Expense ratio and per-unit comparisons

Expense ratio = operating expenses ÷ EGI. Expense per unit = annual operating expenses ÷ unit count.

Why it matters: They help spot omissions but are not universal targets. Climate, size, services, utilities and property age change costs.

Ask: Compare similar assets and inspect each category instead of forcing a generic percentage.

Normalization example: reported NOI is $264,000. Remove a $15,000 nonrecurring recovery from income, add $10,000 of omitted payroll and $8,000 of additional insurance. Revised NOI is $231,000. If a $25,000 roof replacement was included in repairs, it may be moved out of operating expenses for NOI comparison—but it still used cash. Never make a real cash need disappear by changing its label.

Use the general ledger, the detailed list of transactions behind the P&L, to investigate broad labels such as miscellaneous income or maintenance. Verify totals and signs, compare statements with bank activity, and explain rounding differences versus material differences.

CHAPTER 07

Claims, guarantees and subsidized rent

These can support collections, but their labels do not establish their value.

Deposit alternatives and tenant guarantees

A policy, bond, authorization or guarantee may replace or supplement a cash deposit or cover specified rent obligations. Products and contracts differ. A policy limit is the maximum potential coverage under specified terms, not cash held by the property.

Why it matters: A $5,000 policy entry is not a $5,000 deposit, receivable or guaranteed payment. Coverage may exclude the loss you actually experience.

Ask: Who pays, who benefits, what is covered, what are exclusions and deductibles, and can coverage transfer to a buyer?

A claim is not a payment

Separate claimed, submitted, approved, paid, denied and outstanding amounts. Trace each recovery to a unique claim and bank deposit.

Why it matters: A keyword-based spreadsheet can misclassify records. Duplicate rows or overlapping search terms can inflate totals.

Ask: Show original charge, claim identifier, posting date, covered period, remittance date, deposit and any reversals.

Recurring versus one-time recoveries

A recovery may compensate prior unpaid rent, repairs, utilities or legal charges. It may cover an earlier period or a former resident.

Why it matters: Treating a damage recovery as normal new rent may exaggerate steady earnings. Removing recoveries but leaving the associated unusual costs can also distort the picture.

Ask: Which losses does this recovery offset, and what evidence supports future recoveries and related expenses?

Subsidized rent / Section 8

In a housing-choice voucher arrangement, rent can include a resident-paid portion and a housing-agency-paid portion. Approval, contract and inspection requirements apply. [S3]

Why it matters: Do not add assistance to total rent if the total already includes both portions. Interrupted assistance can affect collections.

Ask: What are the approved total rent, tenant share and assistance share? Are contracts current and inspections passed?

How to reconcile a recovery schedule

  1. Read the inclusion rules. Were only positive ledger entries included? Were reversals or refunds omitted?
  2. Check the date range against the T12. A schedule spanning different dates will not automatically match.
  3. Separate rent, damages, utilities, lease-break fees and legal reimbursements.
  4. Deduplicate at the original transaction and claim level; do not sum overlapping keyword totals.
  5. Trace receipts to remittances and bank deposits, then to the P&L classification.
  6. Identify money still owed, disputes and obligations transferred at closing.

So what? Recoveries can be legitimate cash and still be unsuitable as recurring rental income. They may also reveal repeated nonpayment, turnover, resident screening problems or expensive damage.

CHAPTER 08

Buildings, land and the market

The business plan has to match the physical property and the residents who will actually rent it.

Physical condition and capital expenditures (CapEx)

CapEx is major spending to buy, improve or replace long-lived assets, such as a roof or heating system. Routine repair keeps something working; replacement creates a larger cash need. Deferred maintenance is work postponed by the owner.

Why it matters: The price is only part of the investment. Hidden work can consume returns and keep units offline.

Ask: Obtain a property-condition assessment, system ages, repair history, unit inspections, bids and a timed capital budget.

Environmental and safety review

Environmental review investigates contamination and related risks. A Phase I assessment reviews records and site conditions; findings may require further testing. Age and use may warrant lead, asbestos or other specialized reviews.

Why it matters: An attractive building can have expensive conditions a normal tour misses.

Ask: What do reports exclude? Are there unresolved fire, safety, accessibility or environmental issues?

Legal use and ownership

Fee simple generally means ownership of the land and building, subject to legal restrictions and other interests. Ground lease means the land is leased separately. Title, survey, zoning and permits establish ownership, boundaries and authorized use.

Why it matters: An existing apartment count is not proof that every unit is legal or can be rebuilt after damage.

Ask: Verify legal unit count, occupancy permits, title exceptions, easements, parking, flood exposure and legal nonconforming status.

Rent comparables / comps

Comps are competing apartments used to estimate achievable rent. Compare layout, size, condition, amenities, location, utilities and lease terms. Asking rent is an advertised request; achieved rent is what a resident actually agreed to pay.

Why it matters: A renovated large apartment across town may not support the same rent for a small unrenovated unit.

Ask: What rent was recently achieved, with what concessions, at truly similar properties?

Demand and supply

Demand comes from households who need and can afford the units. Supply includes existing competitors and newly delivered apartments. Jobs, wages, household formation and commute patterns inform the picture.

Why it matters: A citywide growth story does not prove demand on the property’s block. Temporary construction crews may leave.

Ask: Who rents here, what can they afford, what is coming online and what remains when temporary demand ends?

Portfolio and replacement cost

A portfolio combines properties. Price per door means price divided by unit count; price per square foot divides by a stated building-area measure. Replacement cost estimates building an equivalent new asset.

Why it matters: A low price per door or below-replacement-cost price does not prove value. Weak earnings or large repairs may explain it.

Ask: Review each property separately. Are square-footage definitions consistent, and is replacement cost relevant to achievable income?

CHAPTER 09

Price, cap rate and value creation

Income supports value—but the market decides what price buyers will pay for that income.

Capitalization rate / cap rate

Cap rate = annual NOI ÷ price or value. At $264,000 NOI and a $4,400,000 price, the cap rate is 6%. Conversely, value = NOI ÷ cap rate.

Why it matters: This is an unlevered operating yield before loan payments, capital spending and taxes, not your investment return. Compare the same NOI definition and time period.

Ask: Is the quoted cap based on historical, current, stabilized or future NOI?

Going-in cap and yield on cost

Going-in cap uses acquisition NOI and purchase price. Stabilized yield on cost uses stabilized NOI divided by total project cost, including acquisition and improvements.

Why it matters: Dividing future NOI only by the purchase price can overstate the yield on the money actually spent.

Ask: Show total cost, time to stabilization and the NOI used in each yield.

Cap-rate expansion and compression

Expansion means buyers require a higher cap rate. Compression means a lower cap rate. With fixed NOI, a higher cap rate produces a lower value.

Why it matters: Operating improvement can be offset by less favorable pricing in the sale market.

Ask: What if the exit cap is higher than expected even after the business plan succeeds?

Value-add

Value-add means improving the property’s cash-generating ability through supported rent changes, occupancy, collections, expense efficiency or improvements.

Why it matters: Opportunity is not free. Renovations cost money, take time and may displace rent.

Ask: What is the cost, downtime, achieved rent increase, ongoing cost and evidence for each improvement?

A small NOI assumption can move the price a lot

At a 6% cap rate, $20,000 of additional recurring NOI implies roughly $333,333 of value ($20,000 ÷ 0.06). If the $20,000 is a one-time recovery, treating it as permanent can greatly overstate value.

$300,000 NOI implies $5 million at a 6% cap, but about $4.286 million at a 7% cap. That is a 14.3% value decline without any fall in NOI.

Do not treat these equations as an appraisal. Buyers also consider condition, location, financing, legal constraints, transaction evidence and the durability of income.

CHAPTER 10

Debt: understand the loan before the return

Debt can amplify gains, but scheduled payments and maturity dates are real obligations.

Loan-to-value (LTV) and loan-to-cost (LTC)

LTV = loan ÷ property value. LTC = loan ÷ eligible project cost. A $3 million loan against $4 million value is 75% LTV. Loan eligibility and the value or cost definition depend on the lender.

Why it matters: A lender may finance less than expected if its appraisal or income assessment is lower.

Ask: What value and costs are used, and what cash fills a funding shortfall?

Interest, amortization and term

Interest is the cost of borrowing. Amortization is the schedule that pays principal down. Term is when the loan matures. A 30-year amortization with a five-year term still requires the remaining balance paid at year five.

Why it matters: A low monthly payment does not mean the debt disappears before maturity.

Ask: What is the monthly payment and remaining balance at maturity?

Interest-only (IO)

During an IO period, payments cover interest without reducing principal. When it ends, payments can increase as principal repayment starts.

Why it matters: Early cash flow can look unusually good while refinance risk remains high.

Ask: Does the forecast include the payment reset and full payoff balance?

Fixed versus floating rate

A fixed rate is set for the defined period. Floating debt changes with a reference index plus a spread. A rate cap may limit a reference rate for a specified period; it has a premium and may need renewal.

Why it matters: A rising rate can consume cash flow. A cap is not automatically protection for the entire loan term or every component of the rate.

Ask: What are the index, spread, floor, reset dates and cap strike, expiry and renewal cost?

Bridge, permanent and agency loans

Bridge debt is generally shorter-term financing for transition. Permanent debt is typically designed for longer-term operations. Agency multifamily programs have specific property and borrower requirements.

Why it matters: The property may need to stabilize before refinancing. Loan availability is not assured.

Ask: What conditions must be met to qualify for the next loan, and what if they are not met by maturity?

Recourse, covenants and prepayment

Recourse may permit claims beyond the property under stated terms. Nonrecourse loans still commonly have specified carve-outs. Covenants are operating or financial requirements. Prepayment charges compensate the lender for early payoff.

Why it matters: Default, early sale or refinance may trigger costs and restrictions absent from the sales model.

Ask: Read guaranties, cash-sweep triggers, extension tests, reserve requirements and prepayment provisions.

DSCR: how much cushion is there?

Debt-service coverage ratio compares available operating earnings with required debt payments. A common broker version is NOI ÷ annual debt service. Lender definitions may use underwritten net cash flow after reserve and other adjustments. Fannie Mae uses underwritten net cash flow for its underwritten DSCR. [S1]

$264,000 NOI ÷ $180,000 annual debt service = 1.47×. That is $1.47 of NOI for each $1 of loan payment. After a $12,000 replacement reserve, a simplified adjusted calculation is $252,000 ÷ $180,000 = 1.40×. At 1.00×, the chosen numerator only covers debt; it does not provide a margin for items excluded from that numerator.

Required minimums vary by lender, program and circumstances. Do not mistake an illustrative target for a universal rule. Check DSCR during lease-up, after IO ends and at refinance—not just in the best stabilized year.

Breakeven occupancy

In a simplified model with fixed costs, full collection and no rent discounts: breakeven occupancy = (operating expenses + debt payments + reserve funding − other income) ÷ full potential annual rent. For $240,000 expenses, $180,000 debt, $12,000 reserves, $24,000 other income and $576,000 potential rent, the result is 70.8%. Adding $20,000 capital work and $5,000 ownership fees raises this version to 75.2%. Actual breakeven depends on collection losses, variable costs, rent mix and timing. State exactly what is covered; this percentage is not a universal safety threshold.

Debt yield and lender sizing

Debt yield = NOI ÷ loan amount. $264,000 ÷ $3,000,000 = 8.8%. It shows earnings relative to lender exposure before the interest rate. Lenders may size debt using the most restrictive of LTV, DSCR, debt yield and other conditions. Meeting one ratio does not guarantee the desired proceeds.

Reserves, escrow and contingency

Operating reserves cover cash shortages. Replacement reserves fund future systems and components. Escrows are money set aside, often with a lender, for specified bills such as taxes and insurance. Contingency is budget for uncertain cost overruns. These are different jobs. A reserve balance is not a recurring expense in itself, but funding it reduces cash you can distribute. Lender-controlled cash may not be freely accessible. [S2]

Do not deduct a roof payment from cash flow and also deduct the same reserve-funded roof withdrawal as another cost. Track funding, balances and actual expenditure separately. Ask whether advertised returns include all equity and whether reserves are sufficient for the capital schedule and downside operating losses.

CHAPTER 12

Returns: learn what each percentage leaves out

A property return and your investor return can be very different.

Cash-on-cash return

Annual cash flow ÷ the defined invested cash. Using $47,000 annual cash available and $2 million initial equity gives 2.35%. State whether the measure is before sponsor allocation and whether it includes fees, reserves and all equity.

Why it matters: It describes annual cash yield, not sale profits, appreciation or the timing of later payments.

Ask: Is this year-one, average or stabilized? Is it property-level or net to my investor class?

Equity multiple

Total cash returned, including capital returned, ÷ total cash contributed. $150,000 received on $100,000 invested is 1.5×, meaning $50,000 total profit before investor taxes under that example.

Why it matters: It does not tell you how long you waited. A 1.5× result over three years differs from one over ten.

Ask: What are the timing, capital calls, fees and actual investor cash flows?

Internal rate of return (IRR)

IRR is the discount rate that makes the present value of the investment’s dated cash inflows and outflows net to zero. It rewards earlier cash receipts. It is not a guaranteed yearly payment.

Why it matters: A large early refinance payout can raise IRR even when long-term risk remains. Unusual cash-flow patterns can make IRR ambiguous.

Ask: Show the complete cash-flow schedule and how much of the forecast return comes from sale or refinance.

Appreciation and principal paydown

Appreciation is an increase in value. Principal paydown reduces debt. Either may increase equity without producing spendable cash today.

Why it matters: Paper equity cannot pay a repair bill unless you access it, which may require a sale or new debt.

Ask: How much return comes from ongoing cash, debt reduction and an assumed future sale?

Taxable income and distributions

Taxable income depends on accounting and tax rules; cash distributions depend on available funds and the agreement. Depreciation allocates eligible costs over time for tax purposes and is not a current cash payment.

Why it matters: A distribution can include return of capital. Tax deductions do not fix a weak operating business.

Ask: What tax reporting will I receive, and how do sale, debt, depreciation and loss limitations affect my situation?

Timing example: invest $100,000 today and receive $150,000 at the end of year five, with nothing in between. Equity multiple is 1.5× and annual IRR is about 8.45%, calculated as 1.51/5 − 1. Receiving the same total sooner increases IRR, but not the equity multiple.

Ask for both levered returns (with debt) and unlevered returns (without debt), and gross versus net investor returns. More debt may increase projected return while also increasing the chance of losing equity.

CHAPTER 13

Partnerships, fees and the waterfall

The waterfall is the agreement’s order for dividing available cash.

Capital stack

The capital stack is the order of funding and claims: commonly senior debt, perhaps subordinate debt or preferred equity, then common equity. Different instruments have different payment priority and rights.

Why it matters: The property might have value while common equity has little left after senior claims.

Ask: Who is ahead of my class, what are they owed, and what can they enforce?

Preferred return / pref

A pref generally gives a class priority to receive a specified return before certain profit splits. It can accrue unpaid amounts if the agreement says so.

Why it matters: A priority is not a guaranteed payment, insurance policy or necessarily debt. Terms determine whether unpaid pref carries forward.

Ask: Is it cumulative, compounded, calculated on outstanding or original capital, and payable from which cash sources?

Promote and profit split

A promote is an additional share of profit awarded to the sponsor under agreed conditions. A 70/30 split commonly means 70% to one group and 30% to another, but the groups and sequence must be defined.

Why it matters: The same property can generate different LP returns depending on fees and waterfall tiers.

Ask: Does the split apply before or after capital return and pref? Is there a catch-up, hurdle or clawback?

Fees and conflicts

Acquisition, asset-management, construction, financing, disposition and property-management fees can apply. Related-party vendors may create conflicts.

Why it matters: Fees can be earned even if investors lose money. Repeated layers reduce net returns.

Ask: List every fee, calculation base, payment timing, recipient and approval process.

Capital calls and dilution

A capital call asks for more money. Dilution reduces an investor’s ownership or economic interest, potentially when new capital is raised or an investor does not fund.

Why it matters: Your initial check may not be your maximum exposure under the documents.

Ask: Are calls mandatory? What happens if I decline? Who approves rescue debt or new equity?

Control, reporting and liquidity

The agreement governs voting, reporting, transfers, manager removal, sale authority and distributions. Private interests can be difficult to resell. [S4]

Why it matters: You may be unable to exit when you want, even if the property is operating well.

Ask: What reports arrive and when? Who can delay sale, withhold distributions or approve affiliate transactions?

A simple waterfall—one specific convention

Assume LPs contribute $1 million. An agreement gives them an 8% annual noncompounding pref on unreturned capital, then splits remaining operating cash 70% to LPs and 30% to the sponsor. There is no catch-up, no sponsor contributed equity, and fees have already been deducted. If year-one available operating cash is $120,000: LPs first receive $80,000; the remaining $40,000 splits $28,000 / $12,000. LPs receive $108,000, sponsor $12,000.

If only $50,000 is available, the LP receives $50,000 and the pref is not fully paid. Whether the $30,000 shortfall accrues depends on the agreement. This illustration does not establish sale proceeds, capital-return priority or the rules for another deal.

A broker OM markets the real estate. A private placement memorandum (PPM) describes an investment offering and its risks; an operating agreement sets ownership rules; a subscription agreement records the investment commitment. Buying partnership interests is different from buying the building directly. Private offerings are not automatically reviewed or approved on their merits by the SEC. [S4]

CHAPTER 14

The business plan and the exit

A forecast needs a path, a budget and a fallback.

Lease-up and stabilization

Lease-up means filling vacant apartments. Stabilization means reaching a supportable steady condition for occupancy, collections and expenses.

Why it matters: Leasing takes marketing, ready units, staff, qualified demand and time. A year-one forecast may silently assume success on day one.

Ask: Show monthly ready units, leases, move-ins, collections, turn costs and cash balances.

Renovation economics

Estimate incremental rent, vacancy during work, renovation cost and any additional ongoing expense. A $100 monthly increase adds $1,200 potential annual rent, before losses and costs.

Why it matters: A beautiful renovation can be a poor investment if residents cannot or will not pay enough more.

Ask: What did completed comparable renovations actually achieve? What is the payback after vacancy and extra costs?

Sale proceeds

Estimate sale price using the stated exit NOI and exit cap rate, then deduct selling costs, loan payoff, prepayment charges and other obligations. Apply the investor waterfall last.

Why it matters: Headline sale price is not the amount equity holders receive.

Ask: Does the model use forward-year NOI or trailing NOI, and are all exit costs included?

Refinance risk

A refinance replaces or changes existing debt. New proceeds may return some equity after payoff and costs, but leave the property with a new obligation.

Why it matters: Returned cash may be borrowed money rather than operating profit. Appraisal, rates and lending standards can prevent the refinance.

Ask: What if new proceeds cannot cover the old loan, and who funds the gap?

Hold period and fallback

The hold period is planned ownership time. A fallback could include delayed improvements or an extended hold, but only if the debt, cash and legal terms allow it.

Why it matters: You cannot simply wait for a better market if a balloon loan is due and extensions are unavailable.

Ask: Can we survive two extra years, and what must happen before the lender’s maturity date?

Sale example: $300,000 exit NOI ÷ 6.5% exit cap = approximately $4,615,385 gross sale price. Less 3% selling costs ($138,462) and a $2.8 million loan payoff leaves about $1,676,923 for equity before other exit obligations and the waterfall. A 7.5% exit cap produces a $4 million price and about $1.08 million after the same cost percentage and debt payoff.

Finish with five short statements: (1) what the property earns now; (2) what must change and its cost; (3) how much cash and debt are needed; (4) what happens in the downside; (5) what evidence is still missing. CHAPTER 19

Your repeatable review workflow

Use the same sequence every time, then deepen the review where the facts require it.

  1. Screen: define asset, price, market, occupied and down units, present collections, rough NOI and obvious repair needs.
  2. Reconcile: align document dates; connect leases to ledgers, ledgers to P&L and P&L to bank evidence. Resolve unusual items.
  3. Build: create an evidence-based operating budget and a monthly transition plan. Include all sources, uses and reserves.
  4. Finance: obtain lender terms and calculate coverage, cash needs, payment resets and balloon payoff.
  5. Inspect: verify physical, environmental, legal and market conditions and price the required work.
  6. Evaluate people and terms: review track record, ownership, fees, voting, distributions and funding obligations.
  7. Stress: test worse collections, higher expenses, delayed work, higher rates and weaker exit pricing together.
  8. Decide and monitor: document unresolved issues and conditions. After acquisition, compare actual results with budget and act before reserves run low.
A deal is understandable when you can explain: where its money comes from, where it goes, what must improve, who is responsible, who gets paid first and what happens if the plan fails.

From offer to closing

An LOI outlines the proposed price, deposit, investigation period, financing conditions and closing date. The purchase agreement controls the binding transaction. Track when earnest money becomes nonrefundable and which contingencies allow termination. During due diligence, verify facts and negotiate remedies before deadlines. At closing, review prorated rents and bills, deposits, delinquent receivables, service contracts, loan funding and the transfer of keys, records and insurance. Arrange staffing and resident communication for day one. A calendar deadline never substitutes for missing evidence.
